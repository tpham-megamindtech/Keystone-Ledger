Several rural Pennsylvania counties are struggling to replace retiring primary care physicians, leaving some patients on waitlists of several months or forcing them to seek care at clinics thirty to forty miles from home, according to data compiled by a state rural health association.

Physician recruiters say rural primary care positions have become significantly harder to fill over the past decade, as medical school graduates increasingly gravitate toward specialties and locations offering higher pay, more predictable hours, and access to professional networks concentrated in larger metro areas.

"We can offer loan repayment and a signing bonus," said a recruiter for a rural health network spanning three counties. "What we can't offer is a spouse's job market or the kind of amenities a young physician with options might want."

Some counties have turned to nurse practitioners and physician assistants to fill primary care gaps, a workaround state scope-of-practice rules generally permit but which some patients and physician groups view as an imperfect substitute for physician-led care. State lawmakers have debated expanding loan forgiveness programs specifically targeted at rural placements, though funding levels remain a point of ongoing budget negotiation.
