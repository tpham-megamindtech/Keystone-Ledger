Several rural Pennsylvania ambulance services built on volunteer staffing models say they can no longer reliably guarantee coverage during all hours of the week, as the number of trained volunteer EMTs continues a decline that has stretched over two decades.

Agency leaders point to the demanding certification requirements and the difficulty of finding volunteers willing to commit to overnight shifts, particularly as more EMTs pursue paid positions at larger, better-resourced services rather than volunteering while working a separate full-time job.

"We used to have three ambulances and enough volunteers to staff all of them around the clock," said the chief of one township's volunteer EMS company. "Now some nights we have coverage for one, and we're relying on mutual aid from neighboring townships more than we ever used to."

Mutual aid agreements between neighboring departments have become the default backstop, though officials warn the arrangement strains every participating agency simultaneously if multiple calls occur at once. Some counties have begun exploring hybrid paid-volunteer staffing models, though funding such positions requires either new local tax revenue or state subsidy that lawmakers have debated without committing to a specific statewide funding mechanism.
