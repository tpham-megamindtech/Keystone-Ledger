Six nail technicians in Philadelphia pooled their savings last year to buy out their former employer's salon after she announced plans to retire, converting the business into a worker-owned cooperative with shared decision-making and profit distribution.

"None of us wanted to start over building a new client base somewhere else," said one of the cooperative's founding members. "Buying the salon we already worked at made more sense than any other option."

The technicians secured a small-business loan through a community development financial institution that specializes in cooperative conversions, a financing niche that has grown as more retiring small-business owners without succession plans look for alternatives to simply closing.

Under the new structure, all six members vote on major business decisions, from pricing changes to hiring, while day-to-day scheduling responsibilities rotate. The group says the transition required setting up formal governance documents none of them had experience with, requiring outside legal help funded partly through a cooperative development nonprofit.

Client retention through the ownership change has been strong, with the salon reporting minimal turnover in its regular customer base.
