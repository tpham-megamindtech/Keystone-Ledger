*Editor's note: This is a fictional case study, written for training and educational purposes for beauty and wellness business owners and staff. The salon, technician, and client described below are not real. Any resemblance to an actual business or individual is entirely coincidental.*

Health code compliance and wound-handling protocol are not customer-service niceties that a busy shift can quietly skip. They are baseline safety requirements, and the moment a technician improvises around them — even briefly, even with good intentions — a routine service can turn into a bloodborne pathogen exposure incident. The following case study, developed for staff training, walks through how one small lapse compounded into a serious health and safety failure, and what it should have looked like instead.

## The Submerged-Wound Incident

At a mid-sized nail studio we'll call Marbella Nail Studio, a technician nicked a client's cuticle with a cuticle knife early in a standard manicure. It was a small cut, but a real one. Instead of pausing the service, the technician continued working, and the client's finger was submerged in the soaking bowl along with the rest of her hand.

No first aid was applied. The service was not stopped. The injury was not disclosed to the client in the moment, and no offer was made to clean the cut, apply pressure, or bandage it before continuing. The client noticed the sting and the visible nick herself partway through the appointment.

This is the point at which the incident stopped being a minor mishap and became a protocol failure. An open cut submerged in a shared soaking bowl — used across multiple clients in a day — is a direct bloodborne pathogen exposure pathway, both for the client whose skin was broken and for anyone else whose skin contacts that water afterward.

## Bare-Hand Contact and a Skin Reaction

Later in the same appointment, while addressing the cut, the technician handled the area with bare hands rather than gloves, despite visible blood being present. No barrier protection was used, and the tools involved were not set aside for disinfection before returning to service.

Within a day, the client reported localized redness, swelling, and irritation at the site of the cut — a skin reaction consistent with a wound that was not properly cleaned, covered, or protected from contaminated surfaces and shared solution during the remainder of the service. The client sought medical attention as a precaution. This is a clinical detail, not a dramatic one: it illustrates exactly why "just a small nick" cannot be treated as cosmetically irrelevant once skin is broken.

## Why This Is a Health Board Matter, Not Just a Service Complaint

It's tempting to file an incident like this under "bad customer experience" — a technician who was too rushed, or too embarrassed, to stop and address a cut properly. That framing understates what actually happened. Across the beauty and wellness industry, standard infection-control practice for any skin-breaking incident during a service is consistent and non-negotiable:

- **Stop the service immediately** once skin is broken, regardless of how minor the injury appears.
- **Apply first aid** — clean the wound, stop any bleeding, and bandage it before any further contact with tools, solutions, or shared basins.
- **Switch to gloves or other barrier protection** for any technician handling an area where blood is present, for the remainder of that interaction.
- **Disinfect or discard** any tool, basin, or surface that contacted blood before it touches another client.

None of these are optional judgment calls left to a technician's discretion mid-appointment. They are baseline bloodborne pathogen precautions that exist because broken skin and shared tools or solutions create a genuine transmission risk — not just an aesthetic or comfort issue. A salon that treats a cut as something to quietly work around, rather than a hard stop, is operating outside standard health and safety practice, and that's the category regulators and health boards evaluate it in.

## Takeaways for Salon Owners

- **Build a mandatory stoppage protocol.** Any visible cut, no matter how small, should trigger an automatic pause in service — not a judgment call by the technician under time pressure.
- **Require gloves the moment blood is present.** Barrier protection isn't just for treating the injury; it applies to the technician for the remainder of that client's service.
- **Train every technician on bloodborne pathogen standards**, not just once at hiring but on a recurring basis, including what counts as an exposure incident and what documentation it requires.
- **Retire or disinfect any tool or basin involved before it touches another client** — never assume a quick rinse is sufficient once blood has been present.
- **Build a clear escalation protocol for communication gaps.** If a technician isn't confident communicating a safety issue in the moment — for any reason, including a language gap between staff and client — there should be a designated bilingual staff member or manager to call in immediately. A client's pain or safety concern should never go unaddressed because the person nearest to them wasn't equipped to communicate it. That's an operational planning failure on the salon's part, not a reflection on the staff member.
- **Document every incident**, however minor it seems in the moment. A written record of what happened and what corrective steps were taken is what separates a salon that takes safety seriously from one that got lucky.

The lesson for owners isn't that mistakes never happen — cuts during nail services are not unusual. It's that the response to a cut is what determines whether an incident stays a minor, well-handled hiccup or becomes a genuine health and safety violation that erodes client trust for good.
